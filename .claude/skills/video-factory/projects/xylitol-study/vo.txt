## COLD OPEN
I'm a public health doctor. For years, I've told people the same thing about sugar-free gum: it's fine. Better than the sugar version. Chew it.
This week I've been reading a study that made me want to check whether I've been giving good advice.
Seventeen thousand people. Up to thirty years. And a sweetener that's in your gum, your toothpaste, your peanut butter and your "sugar-free" everything.
So I read the actual paper. Not the headline. And I want to show you the thing that basically none of the coverage mentioned — because once you see it, you can't unsee it.

## CH 1 — WHAT THEY FOUND
Quick note first: this is my professional read of published research. It isn't medical advice for you specifically. If you've got heart disease, talk to your own doctor. Right — let's go.
Xylitol. It's a sugar alcohol. Tastes almost exactly like sugar, barely touches your blood glucose, made from birch or corn. It's been in gum and toothpaste for decades and dentists genuinely love it, for good reason.
Here's the part that matters later: your body already makes it. Xylitol is a normal byproduct of glucose metabolism. You've got some in your blood right now. But the amounts added to food are over a thousand times higher than the amounts you make.
Last Saturday in Munich, at the world's biggest heart conference, a team from the Charité in Berlin presented this.
Seventeen thousand seven hundred and ten people. Two long-running population studies — one Canadian, one British. Not heart patients. Ordinary people.
They measured xylitol in the blood, sorted everyone into four groups, and waited.
Canadian cohort, six years: the top quarter had a fifty-seven per cent higher risk of dying, or having a heart attack, or a stroke, than the bottom quarter.
British cohort, thirty years: eighteen per cent.
And the middle groups sat in between. More in the blood, more events. That's a dose-response — and that's the pattern that makes epidemiologists take something seriously.

## CH 2 — WHY THIS ONE IS HARDER TO DISMISS
If this feels familiar — it should. Same group published on xylitol in 2024 and it got huge coverage. But that one was easy to argue with. About three thousand people, all already in hospital for cardiac testing, followed for three years. Sick people, short window.
This one answers both. Five times the people. General population. Six years in one cohort, thirty in the other.
And they report the link held after adjusting for body mass index, blood pressure, diabetes and cholesterol. That matters, because the obvious objection is that people who buy sugar-free products are already people with a metabolic problem. If that were the whole story, those adjustments should have made the signal vanish. They say it didn't.

## CH 3 — THE PATTERN
Now widen out, because xylitol isn't the first one.
In 2023, the same lab published this on erythritol — the sweetener in basically every keto product on the shelf. Nature Medicine. Cleveland cohort: eighty per cent higher risk. Berlin cohort: a hundred and twenty-one per cent.
Then xylitol in 2024. Fifty-seven.
Then Munich, this week. Fifty-seven, and eighteen.
Five separate cohorts. Two different sugar alcohols. Two continents. Every single one pointing the same way.
Now — be careful with that chart, including with mine. Those studies don't all use the same comparison groups. Some compare quarters, one compares thirds. So this is a pattern, not a meta-analysis, and anyone showing it to you as if the bars are interchangeable is overselling it. Including me, if I didn't just say that.
But the direction is the direction. And when the same signal turns up in five independent groups of people, that's when you stop calling it a fluke.

## CH 4 — WHY CLOTTING, NOT CHOLESTEROL
So what's the mechanism? It isn't cholesterol. It's clotting.
Platelets are the cells that clump to seal a cut. Brilliant when you're bleeding. Dangerous when it happens inside an artery that's already narrowed — because that is what a heart attack and most strokes actually are.
In the 2024 work, they gave ten healthy volunteers a drink with thirty grams of xylitol in it. Within thirty minutes, several measures of platelet stickiness had gone up.
Same thing showed up with erythritol, in twenty volunteers, at a dose you'd get from one sugar-free soda.
Hold onto that thirty grams. We're coming back to it, and it's the number that decides whether any of this applies to you.

## CH 5 — THE THIRTEEN MINUTES
Okay. This is the part I'd want a colleague to tell me, and almost nobody is reporting it.
Xylitol does not hang around. Its half-life in your blood is about thirteen minutes. After that test drink, levels spiked to as much as a thousand times normal — and were back to baseline within four to six hours.
Thirteen minutes.
Now go back to those cohort studies and ask a boring methods question. When were the blood samples taken?
After an overnight fast.
So by the time that needle went in, anything anyone ate the day before was long gone. Which means the xylitol they measured — the thing that predicted heart attacks — was almost entirely xylitol those people's own bodies were making.
And this isn't me being clever. It's what the researchers wrote themselves, in the 2024 paper. Their words: the plasma levels in their observational cohort represent variations in endogenous production, and not food intake.
That is a very different claim from the one in your feed.
It leaves a real possibility on the table: that something is already going wrong in this person's metabolism, and one of the ways it shows up is more xylitol in their blood. In my field we call that a marker rather than a cause.
A smoke alarm is strongly associated with house fires. It is not what burns the house down.

## CH 6 — THE ARGUMENT BACK
Now, the researchers have an answer to that, and it's a good one — I want to be fair here.
Their argument: yes, the baseline is what your body makes. But what's added to food is over a thousand times higher. So if a small self-made amount tracks with risk, a big dietary spike might be doing more, not less. The fasting number might be understating it.
Other cardiologists have pushed back, in the same journal, under their own names. Their points: xylitol was given intravenously to patients in Germany from the 1970s, at doses way beyond anything you'd eat — and the harm in that record is a rare kidney complication at extreme doses, not heart attacks. The animal work needed xylitol injected, because mice barely absorb it by mouth. And a separate double-blinded study found seven grams of dietary xylitol produced no detectable rise in blood levels at all.
And then the argument I find most interesting, as a public health person. Tooth decay and gum disease are themselves associated with stroke and death. Xylitol reduces both. So at the doses dentists actually recommend, the net effect on your heart might run the other way.
Nobody here is a crank. They're all publishing in the same journal with the data on the table. And one more thing you should know: the senior author on the erythritol work holds patents with his institution on cardiovascular diagnostics. That doesn't make him wrong. It's just something you're entitled to know, and I'd want you to know if the situation were reversed.

## CH 7 — YOUR PACKET, AND THE THING NOBODY CAN CHECK
So let's get practical. Thirty grams was the test dose. What's actually in the stuff you buy?
A piece of xylitol gum: somewhere between a fifth of a gram and one gram. Dentists suggest five to ten grams a day for the cavity benefit. Thirty grams — the amount that moved platelets in a lab — is, in the researchers' own comparison, about a pint of xylitol-sweetened ice cream.
So two pieces of gum is not the experiment. Toothpaste isn't either — you spit it out.
Where you can hit thirty grams without noticing is bulk xylitol swapped one-for-one for sugar in home baking, sugar-free sweets eaten by the bag, and sugar-free ice cream and drinks by the tub. That's the honest line. It's a kitchen-scale exposure, not a pocket-sized one.
Two more things, and these are the ones I actually care about professionally.
First: fifty-seven per cent is a relative number. Fifty-seven per cent more than what? The presentation didn't publish the underlying event rate. So nobody — not me, not any channel running this — can currently tell you what it means in people per thousand. That number matters more than the percentage, and we don't have it.
Second, and this is the one that genuinely bothers me. Look at a label. Sugar alcohols aren't required to be listed individually. So you can buy a product, read the panel properly, do everything right — and still have no way of knowing how much xylitol is in it.
Whatever the cardiology turns out to say, that gap is a problem right now. You cannot make an informed choice about an exposure you're not permitted to measure.

## CH 8 — THE PART THAT ISN'T DEBATED
One thing about xylitol nobody argues about. And it's worth thirty seconds, because unlike everything else in this video, this one is settled and it kills.
Xylitol is severely toxic to dogs.
Dogs absorb it fast and dump three to seven times the normal amount of insulin. Around a tenth of a gram per kilo of body weight can crash a dog's blood sugar within an hour. Half a gram per kilo risks acute liver failure.
For a ten-kilo dog, that first threshold is about one gram. One or two pieces of gum. In a handbag, on a chair, at dog height.
Cats aren't affected the same way. Dogs are. The FDA has warned about this repeatedly.
So while the cardiologists work out the human question, the veterinary one is already answered: sugar-free gum, sugar-free peanut butter and baking xylitol go in a closed cupboard.

## CH 9 — HOW I READ IT
So — has my advice changed?
A bit. Here's my actual read.
The finding is real. Five cohorts, two continents, one direction, and a mechanism that makes biological sense. That's not nothing and I'm not going to wave it away.
But what hasn't been shown is that eating xylitol is what put it in those people's blood. That study hasn't been done. And the design that would settle it is not mysterious — measure what people actually consume alongside what's in their blood. Or run a trial with hard endpoints instead of platelet readings.
So: gum, mints, toothpaste? I'm not worried, and neither are the dentists, and the dental benefit is real and well documented. Baking with it by the cupful, or living on sugar-free tubs and bottles? That's a different exposure, and I'd want to know more before I did that every day — especially for anyone already at high clotting risk.
And the last thing. "Sugar free" was never a health claim. It was a claim about sugar. Saccharin, aspartame, sucralose, erythritol, now xylitol — every one arrived as the safe answer, and every one is still being studied decades later.
The label tells you what isn't in the packet. It was never a promise about what is.
