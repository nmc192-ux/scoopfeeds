# What Happens If The Strait Of Hormuz Closes

Runtime target 8-9 min · 73 beats · ~1,190 words

## STORY SPINE

1. **Through-line object — the ship that cannot move.** A single vessel at
   anchor, returned to in every chapter, escalating: one ship waiting → two
   thousand ships → ships abandoned by their owners → ships nobody can locate.
2. **Question posed early, answered last.** "What happens if the Strait of
   Hormuz closes?" is asked in beat 2 and deliberately not answered until beat
   69. Every chapter offers a candidate answer — a price, an insurance rate, a
   pipeline, a cargo — and each is set aside as insufficient.
3. **The reveal (beats 63-67).** The counts do not agree. Twenty thousand,
   eleven thousand, eight thousand, six thousand — and "many others unaccounted
   for". The remembered moment is not a number. It is that there is no number.
4. **Escalation.** price → insurance → the routes that don't exist → the ships
   → the crews → the deaths → the abandonment → the uncountable.

Framing rule: **strictly neutral on causes.** We state what happened and when,
attributed, and do not adjudicate responsibility for the war. Every argument
rests on a measurable consequence.

## COLD OPEN

1. For forty years, this was a question people only asked as a hypothetical.
2. What happens if the Strait of Hormuz closes?
3. It was a war-game scenario. A chapter in an energy textbook. A line in a risk report that nobody expected to need.
4. On the fourth of March, twenty twenty-six, it stopped being hypothetical.
5. This is what actually happened, and it is not the thing most people were watching.

## CHAPTER 01 — The narrowest place in the world economy

6. The Strait of Hormuz is a bend of water between Iran and Oman.
7. At its tightest, the shipping lanes are about two miles wide in each direction.
8. Every barrel of oil that leaves the Persian Gulf by sea has to pass through that gap.
9. In a normal year, that is around twenty million barrels a day.
10. Roughly a quarter of all the oil that moves by sea anywhere on earth.
11. And close to a fifth of the world's liquefied natural gas.
12. Eighty-four percent of the crude going through it is bound for Asia, not for Europe or America.
13. China draws about a third of its oil through this single bend of water.
14. Europe gets between twelve and fourteen percent of its gas from Qatar, and all of it sails the same route.
15. So when people say the strait matters, this is what they mean. Not a symbol. A bottleneck.

## CHAPTER 02 — The part everyone covered

16. On the twenty-eighth of February, American and Israeli strikes hit Iran.
17. On the fourth of March, Iran declared the strait closed.
18. What happened next was reported everywhere, because it was the easiest part to measure.
19. On the eighth of March, Brent crude passed one hundred dollars a barrel, the first time in four years.
20. It kept climbing, to a peak of one hundred and twenty-six.
21. March twenty twenty-six became the largest single-month rise in the price of oil ever recorded.
22. Then came the second number, the one the shipping industry actually watches.
23. War-risk insurance. What it costs to promise that a ship will arrive.
24. Before the war, covering a tanker through the strait cost about one eighth of one percent of its value.
25. Within nine days, rates had risen four to six times over.
26. For the largest tankers, that worked out to roughly a quarter of a million dollars. Per crossing.
27. And this is usually where the coverage ended. A chart, a price, a forecast, a man in a studio.
28. But a price is what happens to a barrel. It is not what happens to a ship.

## CHAPTER 03 — There is no way around

29. The obvious question is why anyone would keep using a waterway a navy can close.
30. And the Gulf states asked it long before this war. They built the answer out of steel.
31. Saudi Arabia runs a pipeline straight across the country, east to west, to the Red Sea.
32. The Emirates run another, from the Habshan fields to the port of Fujairah, outside the strait entirely.
33. Both exist for exactly this scenario. Both were built to make Hormuz optional.
34. Between them, the spare capacity available is somewhere around three and a half to five and a half million barrels a day.
35. Against the twenty million that normally goes through the water.
36. So the bypasses are real, and they are useful, and at their absolute best they carry about a quarter of the problem.
37. The Emirates are now fast-tracking a second line. It is expected to be running in twenty twenty-seven. That is not help you can use this year.

## CHAPTER 04 — The ships stopped

38. Here is the thing about a tanker that a pipeline map will never show you.
39. A ship is not a truck. It cannot pull over and wait somewhere safe.
40. Once a vessel is inside the Persian Gulf, the only way out is the way it came in.
41. There is no back door. There is no overland route. There is one exit, and it was shut.
42. So when the strait closed, the ships already inside it simply stopped.
43. Not figuratively. They dropped anchor, and they stayed there.
44. By the end of March, the International Maritime Organization was counting about two thousand vessels.
45. Oil tankers. Gas carriers. Bulk carriers. Container ships.
46. And six cruise liners.
47. Some of those ships have now been sitting at anchor for months.

## CHAPTER 05 — The people on them

48. And this is the part that did not make the charts, because it is harder to put on one.
49. Those two thousand ships were not empty.
50. In March, the maritime organization put the number of seafarers stranded aboard them at twenty thousand.
51. Damien Chevallier, who runs the agency's maritime safety division, described it in one sentence.
52. There is no precedent for the stranding of so many seafarers in the modern age.
53. It is worth being precise about who these people are.
54. They are not, for the most part, from the countries arguing over the strait.
55. They are from India. The Philippines. Indonesia. Ukraine. Romania.
56. Filipino sailors alone make up close to a third of the world's crews.
57. The International Transport Workers' Federation received more than a thousand emails from crews aboard those ships.
58. Asking, mostly, one thing. To be brought home.

## CHAPTER 06 — Nobody can count them

59. In July, the United Nations human rights office reported something considerably harder to explain.
60. Some of the shipowners had stopped answering.
61. At least nine vessels, carrying ninety-three crew between them, were abandoned by their owners at Iranian ports.
62. Left aboard, without reliable food, water, medicine, or wages.
63. An evacuation plan did exist. Around twenty-nine hundred people were taken out aboard a hundred and thirty-six ships.
64. Then the plan was paused. It has not restarted.
65. And here the numbers stop agreeing with one another.
66. Twenty thousand stranded in March. Eleven thousand, the agency's own figure for the war as a whole. Eight thousand in July. Six thousand in August.
67. Those cannot all be true, and we are not going to pretend to know which one is.
68. Because the phrase the agency itself keeps using is the part worth sitting with. Many others are unaccounted for.

## CHAPTER 07 — The answer

69. So. What happens if the Strait of Hormuz closes?
70. The honest answer is not a price. The price recovered. Prices almost always do.
71. It is that thousands of people are still sitting at anchor inside somebody else's war,
72. working for companies that in some cases stopped replying, from countries with no seat at the table.
73. And the closest thing we have to a number for them is that nobody has one. That is what closed means.

## DELIBERATELY EXCLUDED

Presented in the film as a contradiction rather than resolved, because the
sources genuinely disagree and choosing one would be a judgement we cannot
support:

- **How many were ever stranded.** UN News reports 20,000 as of 31 March; the
  IMO's own figure cited in July is 11,000 for the entire war. Both are stated,
  in beat 66, and neither is preferred.
- **How many remain.** 8,000 (July) and 6,000 (August) come from different
  reports and may count different classes of vessel.

Excluded entirely, because we could not verify them to a primary source:

- **A "17 seafarers killed" figure** appearing in secondary aggregation
  attributed to the Guardian. We saw 10 deaths as of 31 March and "at least 14
  civilian seafarers" in July, from different outlets on different bases. The
  film therefore states **no** running death toll.
- **~50 Iranian mariners reported dead**, sourced to Iranian statements. Not
  independently verified; excluded rather than attributed to a party to the war.
- **Live transit counts and day-numbers** from commercial trackers ("3-4
  transits a day, down 90%"). Widely repeated but not confirmed against a
  primary shipping-authority release, so no current-traffic figure is asserted.
- **Named individual seafarers and their testimony.** The strongest human
  material sits behind outlets we could not fetch (CNN returned 451, OHCHR 403).
  Rather than paraphrase reporting we have not read in full, the film uses only
  the institutional findings we could verify directly.

## NEUTRALITY NOTE

The film states that strikes occurred on 28 February and that Iran declared the
strait closed on 4 March. It does not assign responsibility for the war, does
not characterise either party's motives, and recommends no action against
either. Every claim it makes is about a measurable consequence.
